Measuring the Stability Assumption Behind Action Chunking

Aryan Goyal
Independent Researcher
garyan18@gmail.com
arXiv Code

Abstract

Action chunking improves the performance of policies learned by behavioural cloning, and several mechanisms have been proposed to explain why, including temporal consistency, horizon reduction, representation learning, and reduced error compounding. Every one concerns the error the policy introduces, whether by reducing how much of it enters or how often it re-enters the policy's own input. We instead study what happens to an action error once it enters the system. At each state, we inject a small action error and measure how fast it grows or shrinks under two execution regimes: open-loop, where the rest of the chunk is replayed without replanning, and closed-loop, where the policy replans after the perturbation. The fitted rate labels each state as contracting, expanding, or unresolved. Across twelve manipulation tasks from three benchmark suites, we find that confidently stable states are rare, while error amplification is common among states whose propagation rate can be resolved. We further find that the measured propagation rate depends strongly on the fitting horizon: amplification is typically front-loaded, so short windows can substantially overestimate longer-horizon propagation. Finally, we train predictors on these labels and find that a state's open-loop regime can be recovered from camera frames and proprioception alone, without a simulator, while its closed-loop propagation is only partially recoverable because it also depends on how the policy acts after the perturbation. These results suggest that error-compounding arguments alone do not provide a complete account of action chunking: neither passive open-loop dynamics nor policy replanning consistently contracts an injected error, and replanning rarely turns open-loop amplification into confident contraction. This also suggests that closed-loop reactivity should be trained explicitly, using perturbation- and tree-coverage-oriented training to expose the policy to deviations it must recover from, rather than expected to emerge reliably from standard imitation learning.

Why measure what happens after an error?

The chunking guarantees rest on a single contraction rate. Is there one?

Imitation-learned manipulation policies increasingly predict chunks of actions rather than single steps. A chunked executor plays \(k\) actions open-loop and then replans, and \(k\) is chosen once per task. Chunked execution is widely reported to outperform single-step execution, but the explanation is contested. The accounts on offer attribute the gain to different mechanisms: temporal consistency, horizon reduction, representation learning, non-Markovian expressivity, delayed conditioning, implicit ensembling, and limited context. Every one of these concerns the error that enters the loop: how often the policy introduces one, or how large it is when introduced. None of them addresses what happens to the error afterwards, and that is arguably the more consequential question, because small deviations are inevitable in learned policies. The introduced error can be amplified, absorbed, or carried forward along the horizon of the task.

The theoretical bounds do speak to this, but they assume a single global contraction rate for the dynamics rather than a rate that varies with state. Zhang et al. (2025) assume the true dynamics are open-loop exponentially incrementally input-to-state stable with a fixed decay factor \(\rho\in(0,1)\), and show that sufficiently long action chunks restore exponential stability of the learned closed loop, with the required chunk length growing as contraction weakens. Lazzati et al. (2026) likewise derive their bound under deterministic Lipschitz dynamics. In both analyses the relevant dynamical constants are assumed rather than measured and treated as global, whereas the propagation of an action error may depend strongly on the state and task geometry. A manipulator in free space and the same manipulator during contact need not propagate an error at the same rate.

Replanning is the other way an error can be dealt with once it has entered, and it raises the same question about the deployed policy rather than the plant. Closed-loop stability is a property of a controller paired with a plant, and the pair that matters here is not the one the theory assumes. The expert paired with the true dynamics, \((\pi^{\star}, f)\), is stable by construction, since a human demonstrator sees the error and corrects it. What determines whether replanning helps at deployment is the learned policy paired with the same dynamics, \((\hat{\pi}, f)\), and stability of the former does not imply stability of the latter. Behaviour cloning fits the expert's actions at the expert's states and constrains little about what the policy does once it has drifted, which is precisely where correction is required. Whether \((\hat{\pi}, f)\) is stable at a given state is an empirical property of the trained policy in its environment, to be measured rather than inherited from the demonstrator. Existing methods that vary the execution horizon at test time do not supply the answer either: they trigger on model confidence, attention weights, action entropy, denoising variance, or agreement between chunks, each a proxy for how certain the policy is about its own output rather than a measurement of how the plant treats an action error.

What we do

Label every state on two axes

We label, per state, how an injected action error propagates over the steps that follow: what the plant does with it while the policy executes a chunk blind, and what the deployed policy does with the same error when it is allowed to replan. The two correspond to the decision the executor makes at that moment, since a longer chunk leaves the deviation to the dynamics and a shorter one hands it to the policy, so the labels say which of the two is doing the work at each state. This lets us ask whether the assumption the chunking guarantees rest on, that the plant absorbs a small error before the next replan, actually holds along the states these policies encounter.

We report the labels across robomimic, MimicGen, and RoboCasa, spanning twelve tasks and two policy classes, at demonstration states, at states the policy visits, and on the chunks the policy itself plans, and then ask whether the regime is recoverable from what a robot can observe.

The measurement

A per-state, finite-horizon probe

We ask a simple question: starting from here, if the executed action is slightly wrong, does the resulting deviation grow or shrink over the steps that follow? The incremental-stability bounds motivate an exponential form: for a small initial deviation they describe propagation of the form \(|\Delta y_j| \propto \rho^j|\Delta y_0|\), so the logarithm of the deviation should vary approximately linearly with the number of steps. We therefore use a local exponential model rather than assuming a global contraction factor. Writing \(y_j\) for a task metric evaluated \(j\) steps after the state along a nominal execution and \(y_j^{(n)}\) for the same quantity along the \(n\)-th perturbed branch,

\[\log \lVert y_j - y_j^{(n)} \rVert \;\approx\; \alpha_n + \lambda_n j,\]

where \(\alpha_n\) absorbs the initial magnitude of the perturbation and \(\lambda_n\) is the local error-propagation rate, playing the role of \(\log\rho\): a positive value indicates growth of the deviation, a negative value indicates decay, and a value near zero indicates that the deviation is approximately preserved. We repeat this fit across \(N\) perturbed branches and aggregate their slopes to obtain the mean rate \(\bar{\lambda}\) and its standard error. Estimating \(\lambda\) requires a window \(K\) over which to fit it, which is a property of the measurement rather than of the executed chunk.

Open-loop. Since this is a property of the dynamics and task geometry rather than of a policy, no trained policy is required to measure it. At a state \(s_t\) we replay the recorded actions without replanning, perturbing only the translation components of the first action with Gaussian noise of scale \(\sigma_u\), set to the trained policy's action prediction error on held-out demonstrations so that the probe reflects an error of the size the policy makes. The remaining actions are replayed unchanged, isolating how an initial action error propagates through the dynamics.

Closed-loop. This is a property of the plant together with the deployed policy, and therefore requires one. We use the same perturbation construction with one key difference: the policy replans at every subsequent step after the initial perturbation, so both the nominal and perturbed branches are policy-driven. Because the policy may be stochastic, paired branches use common random numbers: within each pair, the two branches use the same sequence of policy random variables, so the measured separation reflects the injected perturbation rather than a different random sample.

Deciding a label

Two gates, three classes

The same two gates are applied to both regimes. The first asks whether the measured rate is large enough to represent meaningful amplification or decay, requiring \(|\bar{\lambda}| \geq \delta_m\), where \(\delta_m = \ln(m)/K\) is the rate corresponding to an \(m\)-fold change over the \(K\)-step window. The second asks whether the sign of the rate is sufficiently well resolved, requiring \(|\bar{\lambda}| \geq t\,\mathrm{SE}\). A stamp that passes both gates is labelled stable when \(\bar{\lambda}<0\) and unstable when \(\bar{\lambda}>0\); stamps that fail either gate are assigned to the deadband.

Deadband is a statement about measurement resolution, not a claim that the true rate is zero.

Results

Neither execution regime reliably contracts an injected error

Across all three stamp types, unstable states are more common than stable states among measurements that clear the gates, while most stamps fall in the deadband. The deadband does not imply stability: it arises when the measured rate is either too small to exceed the amplification threshold or too uncertain to resolve its sign. Open-loop execution therefore does not generally avoid error growth through passive contraction.

Nor is the large deadband primarily a consequence of statistical uncertainty. Among stamps that clear Gate 1, 90–99% also have a sufficiently resolved sign at \(K{=}24\); what drives the deadband is rates too small to cross the amplification threshold. On lift, only 543 of 2,485 demonstration stamps clear Gate 1, so 78% have \(|\lambda| < 0.0289\). Most states do not exhibit confidently measurable amplification or contraction at the chosen threshold, even though the resolved subset is predominantly unstable.

Within robomimic demonstrations, the median propagation rate increases from can (\(0.009\)) to lift (\(0.015\)), square (\(0.017\)), and tool hang (\(0.026\)). This ordering is consistent with a trend toward more contact-sensitive and precision-demanding manipulation: the largest rate occurs on tool hang, where successful execution requires high-precision insertion. These medians do not establish that task difficulty itself causes the increase.

Closed-loop stability is substantially rarer than passive contraction under the same perturbation measurement. Across robomimic, MimicGen, and RoboCasa, the labels contain only a small number of confidently stable stamps: at \(m{=}2\), the stable class holds only 3–88 stamps per task, despite hundreds of non-stable stamps. In this sense, closed-loop execution can be worse than open-loop commitment: it trades away the temporal commitment of a chunk for reactivity without reliably reducing error propagation.

The joint labels then ask whether replanning changes the propagation of an injected error that would otherwise grow during open-loop execution. Across all three benchmark families, the [OL unstable, CL stable] cell is near zero: states with error amplification under open-loop execution rarely show contraction when the policy is allowed to replan. Instead, these states are more often classified as deadband or remain unstable under closed-loop execution. Within the measured propagation horizon, replanning does not generally convert error growth into error contraction. The two labels characterise complementary modes of propagation, and the results show that neither regime can be assumed to attenuate a small error. This finding concerns error propagation only; it does not by itself establish which execution regime yields better task performance or determine an optimal chunk length.

The outlined cell is the clearest case in which the measurements provide actionable evidence, and it is the cell that turns out to be nearly empty. These results also expose a gap in standard imitation training. The task-specific diffusion policies are trained directly from expert demonstrations, without perturbation augmentation or explicit recovery supervision. Such training constrains the policy primarily on the demonstrated state distribution, but provides little supervision for recovery after a deviation. This suggests that closed-loop contraction may need to be encouraged explicitly by training on perturbed states and their corresponding recovery actions, consistent with prior work on noise and perturbation injection, including DART (Laskey et al., 2017) and Simchowitz et al. (2025), where injecting perturbations during training exposes the policy to off-demonstration states and reduces compounding error. Closed-loop stability is unlikely to emerge reliably from imitation learning alone.

Results · the window

The fitting horizon changes the measured rate

The diffusion policies on robomimic and MimicGen emit eight-action chunks, so their open-loop divergence can only be fit over \(K{=}8\), whereas \(K{=}24\) is the primary window for demonstration and rollout stamps because it provides a longer and more stable fit. To compare all three state sources on a common basis, we refit the same stored per-branch log-distance matrices for demonstration and rollout stamps at \(K{=}8\). No additional simulation is required: the two columns contain the same stamps, evaluated over different portions of the same trajectories.

Across datasets and state sources, shortening the window from \(K{=}24\) to \(K{=}8\) moves a large fraction of stamps from the deadband into the unstable class, while the stable fraction changes little. On robomimic lift demonstrations, the unstable share increases from 21.1% at \(K{=}24\) to 69.7% at \(K{=}8\), while the stable share remains 0.0%. The same qualitative shift occurs across the other robomimic, MimicGen, and RoboCasa conditions. Thus, the conclusion that open-loop contraction is uncommon is robust to the choice of window, but the size of the unstable population is not.

This dependence arises because the divergence is typically front-loaded. The perturbed branches separate rapidly during the first few steps and then tend to flatten rather than continue growing at the same rate. A short window therefore fits primarily the initial rise and produces a larger positive \(\lambda\), whereas a longer window averages that rise with the subsequent flat region and pulls \(\lambda\) toward zero. Because the later trajectory does not generally reverse the initial separation, the additional steps move stamps predominantly into the deadband rather than into the stable class. Accordingly, \(\lambda\) should be interpreted as a summary of error propagation over the specified fitting interval, rather than as a time-invariant rate governing the entire trajectory.

This distinction is important when relating the measurement to chunk length. At \(K{=}8\), the probe captures the error growth that occurs immediately after an action perturbation, the short-horizon cost of committing to a chunk; at \(K{=}24\), it also captures the subsequent period in which the task geometry limits further separation. Read against execution, a short open-loop chunk may terminate while the error is still growing, whereas a longer chunk continues through the subsequent plateau and amortises the same initial transient over more executed steps. Consequently, when considering the error incurred by a single chunk, a shorter chunk can actually be less favourable than a longer one. If the initial amplification is repeatedly followed by another chunk boundary before the divergence has reached its saturated regime, multiple short chunks need not benefit from the stabilisation observed at longer horizons at all. Our measurements do not establish this accumulation effect directly, since each probe introduces a single perturbation and measures its subsequent evolution; they show why the relationship between chunk length and error propagation cannot be inferred from the short-window growth rate alone. Claims about the fraction of unstable states must therefore always be stated together with the fitting window.

The result also clarifies how the contraction-based lower bound of Zhang et al. should be interpreted. Their guarantee requires \(k \geq k^{\star} = \log(1/\rho)^{-1}\,\log\big(\mathrm{poly}(L, C_{\mathrm{ISS}})\big)\), where the contraction rate \(\rho\) determines the required chunk length. Our measurements provide an empirical qualification of this picture: errors can amplify during the initial portion of execution without continuing to amplify over the full chunk horizon, so whether a state appears unstable depends not only on the state itself but also on how long the system is observed after the perturbation. We do not interpret the window result as establishing an optimal chunk length or as validating the bound.

Prediction

Can the regime be read from what the robot observes?

Since the open-loop rate is determined by the dynamics and task geometry rather than by policy feedback, it is a property of the state the robot is in, which makes it reasonable to ask whether it is visible: does an observation of the current state, or a short history of observations, carry enough evidence to say what the next several steps will do with an injected error? Both predictor variants use a frozen V-JEPA 2 ViT-L encoder over a short window of camera frames ending at \(t\), together with proprioception over the same window. A learned query attends over the visual tokens, a small recurrent network encodes proprioception, and a two-layer head predicts both a continuous estimate of \(\lambda\) and a logit for the unstable class. The encoder is never fine-tuned, so the variants differ only in their inputs: P receives frames and proprioception only, testing whether the regime is recoverable from the observed configuration; A1 additionally receives the action chunk under evaluation, since \(\lambda\) is defined for the specific actions that are replayed. Predictors are trained separately for demonstration, rollout, and policy stamps, with train and validation sets split by demonstration rather than by stamp, and three random initialisations per cell. Classification is trained and evaluated only on stamps that clear both gates; regression uses all stamps.

Open-loop stability exhibits measurable state-dependent structure that can be partially recovered from observation. Across the demonstration and rollout stamps, the camera-and-proprioception predictor achieves AUROC values between \(0.63\) and \(0.94\) on cells with sufficient negative-class support, with the strongest results on tool_hang and mg-coffee. The observed configuration contains information about whether a small action error is likely to amplify or decay, even without access to the simulator or the actions used in the probe.

The regression is weaker than the classification. Measured against a constant predictor that outputs the training-set median for every stamp, the head recovers between a tenth and a quarter of the error, and on can it does not improve on that baseline at all. The model is better at identifying which states are more prone to error amplification than at predicting how much the error will amplify: observations contain enough information to distinguish stability regimes, but not enough to accurately reconstruct the propagation rate itself. Adding the action sequence generally improves the prediction of \(\lambda\), particularly in regression error and rank correlation, as expected because the measured open-loop rate depends on the particular sequence of actions being replayed. The improvement is not uniform across classification metrics, so we view A1 as providing additional information about the measured execution rather than as uniformly improving regime classification.

The closed-loop rate depends on both the current state and the behaviour of the deployed policy after the perturbation. Nevertheless, because the labels are generated under the same policy used at deployment, the rate may still contain predictable structure tied to the observed configuration. We therefore treat closed-loop prediction as a controlled target ablation: the predictor architecture, inputs, and policy-replan stamps are unchanged, while the target is switched from the open-loop to the closed-loop propagation rate.

The closed-loop rate is partially recoverable from these inputs. Across the evaluated tasks and input variants, the predictor improves over the constant-median baseline on most regression cells, while AUROC exceeds \(0.70\) in half of the cells for which both classes are present. The strongest results occur on lift and mg-coffee, whereas performance is weaker and closer to chance on several of the remaining tasks. The classification results should be interpreted cautiously because the stable closed-loop class is very small at \(m{=}2\), with only a few to tens of stable stamps per task and several single-class validation splits. The result is not that closed-loop stability is purely unpredictable; rather, some state-dependent structure is recoverable, but substantially less uniformly than for open-loop propagation. This is consistent with closed-loop stability depending not only on the configuration but also on the policy's subsequent behaviour.

Stamp sources

Demonstrations, policy rollouts, and the policy's own chunks

The open-loop probe is evaluated at three stamp types, differing in the state distribution and the source of the replayed actions. Demonstration stamps use recorded demonstration states and actions; rollout stamps use states reached by the policy and the actions it executed; and policy stamps use replan boundaries and the action chunk emitted by the policy. This gives two controlled comparisons: demonstration versus rollout changes the state distribution, while rollout versus policy changes the action source. Closed-loop probes are evaluated at the same policy stamps, allowing the two execution regimes to be compared at identical states.

We label three benchmark families, all built on robosuite and MuJoCo, covering twelve tasks and two policy classes, with both human and generated demonstrations. robomimic: four proficient-human teleoperation tasks, lift, can, square, and tool_hang, spanning simple pick-and-place through high-precision insertion, with per-task diffusion policies. MimicGen: demonstrations generated by transforming and replaying human source segments into new object poses; coffee and nut_assembly carry trained diffusion policies and therefore the rollout, policy, and closed-loop axes, while square_d0 and stack_d0 are demonstration only, with a proxy \(\sigma_u\) taken from a robomimic policy. RoboCasa: four kitchen tasks, OpenCabinet, OpenDrawer, PickPlaceCounterToCabinet, and TurnOnSinkFaucet, each with 200 demonstrations, driven by the public GR00T N1.5 checkpoint, a multitask generalist that we do not train. There is no separate rollout corpus on this family, so its demonstration-to-policy comparison has no intermediate control.

Rollout medians are close to their demonstration counterparts (lift \(0.019\) vs. \(0.015\); tool hang \(0.021\) vs. \(0.026\)), suggesting that moving from expert states to policy-visited states does not substantially change how the plant propagates a small error. The three sources also let us test whether policies simply choose to commit at states that are naturally stable: if that were the case, policy-chunk stamps should contain more stable and fewer unstable states than the broader state distributions. At the matched \(K{=}8\) window they do not. Policy stamps are predominantly unstable on robomimic and MimicGen, 71.7% on lift and 70.1% on tool hang, and the stable share remains small across all three stamp types.

Conclusion

What state-wise measurement changes about the stability story

We studied what happens after an action error enters a chunked imitation-learning system, rather than how often the policy introduces one. By measuring error propagation from individual states under both open-loop execution and closed-loop replanning, we find that confidently stable states are uncommon, while amplification is common among states for which the propagation rate is resolved. Replanning frequently removes confident amplification, but rarely produces confident contraction, suggesting that feedback does not generally turn an injected deviation into a reliably shrinking one.

The measurements also show that error propagation is strongly state- and horizon-dependent. Shorter fitting windows emphasise the initial amplification of a perturbation, while longer windows include subsequent saturation, making the measured rate a finite-horizon quantity rather than a single global stability constant. More importantly, even within a single task, propagation rates vary substantially across states, so the assumption of a single global contraction rate is not supported empirically even at the task level, let alone across an entire benchmark dataset.

These results do not determine the best execution horizon by themselves: stability is one factor alongside task performance, temporal consistency, replanning cost, and other effects of action chunking. They instead provide a state-dependent measure of the error-propagation consequences of continuing a chunk versus replanning. Taken together, they suggest that closed-loop error recovery should not be expected to emerge reliably from standard imitation learning. If such recovery is desired, it should be trained explicitly by exposing the policy to perturbations and recovery trajectories through perturbation- and tree-coverage-oriented training.

Method

Measuring open- and closed-loop propagation per state

Show the labeling procedure

We ask a simple question: starting from here, if the executed action is slightly wrong, does the resulting deviation grow or shrink over the steps that follow? Writing \(y_j\) for a task metric evaluated \(j\) steps after the state along a nominal execution and \(y_j^{(n)}\) for the same quantity along the \(n\)-th perturbed branch, we model the local propagation of the deviation as

\[\log \lVert y_j - y_j^{(n)} \rVert \;\approx\; \alpha_n + \lambda_n j,\]

where \(\lambda_n\) is the fitted error-propagation rate for branch \(n\) over the measurement window. A positive \(\lambda_n\) indicates growth of the deviation, so an initial error \(\epsilon\) would grow approximately as \(\epsilon e^{\lambda_n k}\) over a \(k\)-step window; a negative \(\lambda_n\) indicates decay, while a \(\lambda_n\) near zero indicates that the deviation is approximately preserved. We repeat this fit across \(N\) perturbed branches and aggregate their slopes to obtain the mean rate and its standard error. Estimating \(\lambda_n\) requires a window over which to fit it, written \(K\), which is a property of the measurement rather than of the executed chunk.

Open-loop stability. Since this is a property of the dynamics and task geometry rather than of a policy, no trained policy is required to measure it. At a state \(s_t\), we replay the recorded actions without replanning, perturbing only the translation components of the first action with Gaussian noise of scale \(\sigma_u\). We set \(\sigma_u\) to the action prediction error of the trained policy on held-out demonstrations, providing a perturbation magnitude representative of the policy's typical action error. The remaining actions are replayed unchanged, isolating how an initial action error propagates through the dynamics.

Closed-loop stability. Closed-loop stability is a property of the plant together with the deployed policy and therefore requires a trained policy. We evaluate it using the same perturbation construction as in the open-loop setting, with one key difference: the policy replans at every subsequent step after the initial perturbation, so both the nominal and perturbed branches are policy-driven. Replaying the demonstrator actions on either branch would confound the effect of perturbation propagation with the discrepancy between the learned policy and the demonstrator. Because the policy may be stochastic, we use common random numbers: for each initial state we generate \(N\) paired nominal and perturbed rollouts, and within each pair the two branches use the same sequence of policy random variables. The closed-loop rate and its standard error are obtained by averaging the branch-wise rates and using their sample variation. This paired construction isolates the effect of the injected perturbation without estimating or subtracting a separate stochastic-control rate.

State and action sources. We evaluate the open-loop probe at three stamp types, differing in the state distribution and the source of the replayed actions. Demonstration stamps use states from recorded demonstrations and the corresponding demonstrator actions; rollout stamps use states reached by the policy and the actions it executed; and policy stamps use replan boundaries and the action chunk emitted by the policy there. This gives two controlled comparisons: demonstration versus rollout changes the state distribution, while rollout versus policy changes the action source. The fitting window is \(K{=}24\) for demonstration and rollout stamps. For policy stamps, \(K\) is capped by the emitted chunk length: 8 actions for the diffusion policies and up to 24 on RoboCasa. Closed-loop probes use the same policy-replan stamps, so open- and closed-loop rates can be compared at identical states; the closed-loop policy replans at each step and is measured over \(K{=}24\).

Deciding a label. We apply the same two gates to both regimes. First, we ask whether the measured rate is large enough to represent meaningful amplification or decay, comparing \(|\bar{\lambda}|\) against \(\delta_m = \ln(m)/K\), the rate corresponding to an \(m\)-fold change over the \(K\)-step measurement window. Second, we ask whether the sign of the measured rate is sufficiently well resolved, comparing \(|\bar{\lambda}|\) against \(t\,\mathrm{SE}\):

\[\text{Gate 1: } |\bar{\lambda}| \geq \delta_m = \ln(m)/K, \qquad \text{Gate 2: } |\bar{\lambda}| \geq t\,\mathrm{SE}.\]

A stamp that passes both gates is labelled stable when \(\bar{\lambda}<0\) and unstable when \(\bar{\lambda}>0\); stamps that fail either gate are assigned to the deadband. The main text uses \(m{=}2.0\), \(t{=}2.365\), and \(K{=}24\) as the default settings.
