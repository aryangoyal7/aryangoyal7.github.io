In short

Realistic nodules, with control over what they look like

Detecting lung nodules on chest X-rays is hard, partly because detectors don't see enough examples of every kind of nodule. We train a diffusion model that paints a nodule into any chosen spot of a real X-ray, and lets you choose its shape and size (through a mask), its border, its texture, whether it is calcified, and how subtle it is.

Each characteristic is learned by its own small add-on module (a LoRA adapter). To combine two characteristics in one nodule, we train the adapters with an extra loss that keeps them from interfering with each other. Adding the generated nodules to a detector's training data improves detection on in-house and public test sets.

Why separate adapters instead of one conditioning signal

A natural idea is to feed the characteristic label into the model with classifier-free guidance (CFG), the same way the mask is fed in. In practice, guiding on both the mask and the labels made them compete and blurred the nodule's boundary. So the mask keeps CFG, and each characteristic gets its own LoRA adapter. The adapters follow their characteristic much more closely (lower FID is better):

A LoRA adapter freezes the model's weights \(W_0\) and learns a small low-rank update \(\Delta W = AB\) (rank 32 here). Each adapter adds about 6.2M parameters, under 1% of the frozen model, and sits in the attention layers of all 28 transformer blocks.

Subtlety

A slider for how hard a nodule is to see

Radiologists graded each training nodule from 1 (most subtle) to 5 (most obvious). Rather than training one adapter per grade, we tie the grade to the adapter's strength \(\alpha\): subtle nodules are trained with a small \(\alpha\) and obvious ones with a large \(\alpha\),

\[ W = W_0 + \alpha(s)\,\Delta W, \qquad \alpha(s) = 2^{2+s}, \quad s \in \{1,\dots,5\}. \]

At generation time, turning \(\alpha\) down makes the same nodule fainter. Try it on one mask:

Radiologists were shown sets of three such versions and asked to order them from most obvious to most subtle. The panel's majority order matched ours in 80% of sets. Subtle nodules also helped the detector most where it was weakest; see the subtle-nodule results.

Two characteristics at once

Combining adapters without interference

Real nodules rarely have just one trait. A benign nodule is often calcified and smooth; a worrying one is often irregular and uneven in texture. So we want to switch on two adapters at the same time.

Problem 1: they fight over the same pixels

In natural images, a "style" adapter and an "object" adapter usually act on different parts of the image. Here every adapter acts on the same small nodule region, so their updates compete. One trait tends to dominate, and artifacts appear near the mask edge.

Problem 2: their updates overlap

Adapters trained separately end up pointing in similar directions in weight space. Most of an adapter's weights are near zero (60–70% below 0.01), so the few that matter collide when you average two adapters. This hurts every merging method we tried: linear merging, switching and ZipLoRA.

Our fix is to train the two adapters together and add a penalty whenever their updates overlap:

\[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{orth}} = \big\lVert W_1 W_2^{\top} \big\rVert_F^2 \]

The penalty is zero when the two updates are orthogonal, meaning they change the model along independent directions. With it, simply averaging the adapters works, and each trait stays adjustable through its own \(\alpha\).

A fair worry is that the loss just shrinks the adapters toward zero, which would also make the overlap small. It doesn't: the jointly trained adapters actually have larger weights than separately trained ones, so the loss is separating their directions rather than switching them off.

Results

Does it help?

We check three things: whether radiologists find the nodules realistic and correctly characterised, whether the nodules improve a detector, and how the model compares with other nodule generators.

Three experienced radiologists took three blinded tests. Results can be counted two ways: majority vote (the panel's decision per image) or pooled (every individual answer counted). Whiskers show 95% confidence intervals.

Count
Takeaway: most generated nodules pass as real, and radiologists recognise the intended trait in 80–100% of cases by majority vote. Calcification is the hardest trait to read reliably.

A detector (Swin-Tiny encoder with a U-Net++ head) is trained on 10k real nodules and about 100k normal X-rays, plus a growing number of generated nodules. Performance is measured on three test sets.

Metric
Takeaway: every amount of generated data raises AUC over real data alone on all three test sets. Gains level off or dip past a point: on in-house segmentation, 10k generated nodules ends slightly below the baseline (0.306 vs. 0.309 IoU). So more synthetic data is not always better.

All generators were trained on the same in-house data. We compare two GANs (ACGAN, ReACGAN) and CR-Fill, the best inpainting method in the NODE21 generation challenge.

Data and cost

Datasets

The in-house data has 1.2M frontal chest X-rays from partner hospitals, about 40k of them with nodules. Every nodule has a shape outline and characteristic labels from three experienced radiologists. JSRT and ChestX-ray14 are public test sets; their nodule boxes were refined into outlines with our segmentation model and checked by radiologists.

Compute

  • Backbone: DiT-XL/2 (about 680M parameters, 28 transformer blocks) on 256×256 patches, encoded to 32×32 latents. Stage 1 took about a week and Stage 2 about two days, each on four NVIDIA L40 GPUs.
  • Adapters: about 10 hours per characteristic on four L40s; 20–30 hours for a jointly trained pair.
  • Generation: 250 DDIM steps, about 10 seconds per nodule.

Limitations

Some combinations of adapters produce nodules outside what the model saw in training. We evaluate pairs of characteristics only. The loss extends to three or more adapters by penalising every pair, but the data has too few nodules labelled with several traits at once for a fair test, and many three-way combinations are rare in practice.
